Dakota — thank you for reaching out about the former-resident balances at SLS Communities and the Hampton multifamily properties.

You asked seven specific questions, so I’ll answer them rather than send a brochure. Before I do, one thing worth saying up front, because it is usually the real question behind the other seven: your communities sell trust to adult children before they sell anything to a resident, and how a past-due balance is handled after move-out is part of that reputation. These accounts rarely need pressure. They need patience, and someone who remembers the family may have buried a parent six weeks ago.

1. Fee / contingency rate. Pure contingency. No upfront cost, no retainer, no monthly fee, no per-account fee, and nothing at all on what we don’t collect. You pay a pre-agreed percentage only of what we actually recover. The rate follows the age, balance size and volume of what you place, so I’ll quote you a real number once I’ve seen a representative sample of your aging rather than guess at it in an email.

2. Senior living and deceased residents. Independent and assisted living balances are a standing part of our residential book — monthly service fees, rent through the notice period, community-fee shortfalls, damage and restoration charges. Deceased-resident accounts run on a separate, slower track: we confirm the death before any further contact, suspend outreach through the early weeks of loss, and move the account toward the estate rather than toward the family’s front door.

3. Estates, probate and responsible parties. We locate the personal representative and present the claim into the estate, not to relatives who owe nothing. Arizona’s non-claim rules put a hard clock on creditor claims, so these accounts get triaged first — the ones where a resident died some time ago are the most time-sensitive thing in your file. Where a third party signed the residency agreement, we read the actual document: a genuine personal guaranty is pursued on its terms; a signature given purely in a representative capacity is not, and I’d rather tell you that than bill you for working an account that can’t be collected.

4. Eviction, judgments and legal escalation. Yes. Post-eviction former-resident balances are standard work for us, and an existing judgment is an asset — we go straight to post-judgment enforcement through our attorney network instead of starting over. Balances with no judgment are assessed for suit on documentation and asset picture, and nothing is filed without your written approval.

5. Onboarding and what we’d need. Low-lift. A signed agreement, then a spreadsheet export from your property-management system: resident name, last known address, phone, email, dates of residency and move-out, itemized balance, and the community as creditor of record. Residency and lease agreements, final statements, ledgers, and any death notice or eviction order upload into the same secure client portal. We map your columns, so nothing gets re-keyed, and outreach begins within 24–48 hours of a documented placement.

6. Typical timelines. Most of what is going to resolve resolves inside 90–180 days of placement, with first payments commonly in the first 30–60. Estate accounts run longer and follow the court’s calendar rather than ours.

7. Credit reporting. Yes, on eligible accounts. We furnish to Equifax and Experian, and rental and residency balances also go to Experian RentBureau — the tenant-screening database other housing operators pull before approving an applicant, which on a former-resident book is often the single most effective lever you have. Reporting is elected once at onboarding for the portfolio rather than account by account, and nothing is furnished until the consumer has received a validation notice and the dispute window has run.

I’ve put together a capabilities overview built specifically around a senior living and multifamily former-resident book — the case studies in it are real SWRS results from senior living communities and apartment operators, anonymized, and they run from 67% recovered down to 13%. I’ll be straight with you about where your file is likely to land: a book carrying deceased residents and post-eviction balances behaves like the lower half of that range, not the top. The honest read is that the 36 accounts you have now are worth placing, and the larger win is a standing trigger — everything placed at 90 days past move-out, automatically — because recovery drops steeply with age and on contingency there is no cost to placing early. We’ve collected millions of dollars in industries just like yours since 2004.
