01 — Introduction

Caring for a family doesn’t end when the service does

To the team at Reid’s New Golden Gate Funeral Home — thank you for reaching out about help with funeral home collections. Families come to you on the worst day of their lives, and you take care of them first and talk about money later. That is the right order, and it is why people in Milwaukee and Racine trust you with their mother, their husband, their child. It also leaves your office holding balances that were never really about the money: the family who fully intended to settle once the insurance paid, the estate that took far longer than anyone expected, the son who stopped returning calls because he is ashamed he cannot pay yet. Those accounts rarely need pressure. They need patience, and someone with enough distance from the funeral itself to keep the conversation open without asking your directors to stand between a family and its grief.

Behind every aged balance is a person who is still grieving. That is the whole difficulty. The casket, the vault, the transport, the staff hours and the cemetery fees were paid out of your pocket months ago — and the only way to ask about them is to reach back into the hardest season of someone’s life. Your directors carry that weight, families sometimes hear the call as a bill arriving in the middle of mourning, and the balance quietly ages toward a write-off nobody ever decided to take.

Southwest Recovery Services has worked receivables on pure contingency since 2004, and we are a family-owned firm ourselves. We take these accounts slowly, we listen before we ask, and we work with people rather than at them — because a family that is treated with patience usually finds a way to pay, and a family that is treated badly tells the whole neighborhood. You place accounts through a secure portal that takes your export, and we carry the conversation from there. No recovery, no fee.

03 — Your questions, answered

The questions a funeral director should ask before trusting anyone with these families

Honest answers on cost, on how your families will be spoken to, on insurance assignments and estates, and on what placement actually asks of your staff.

Your questionSouthwest Recovery Services
1. What does it cost?Pure contingency — $0 upfront, no retainer, no monthly minimum. You pay a pre-agreed percentage only of what we actually recover. The rate is quoted once we see a representative sample of your past-due accounts, and it scales with the age, balance and volume you place.
2. How will my families be treated?This is the question that matters most, and we would rather you judge us on it than on a recovery rate. Every contact begins by listening. If someone is still in the early weeks of loss, or waiting on a policy, or simply not able yet, we slow down and work out a path rather than press. No threats, no misrepresentation, no pressure tactics — ever. FDCPA and Reg F requirements are enforced inside the workflow and every conversation is documented, so that if a family ever calls your office upset, you can see exactly what was said and when. Your name is the one that has to stay good in that neighborhood.
3. Insurance assignments & estatesA family waiting on a life-insurance assignment, a probate estate or a Title 19 determination is not a family refusing to pay, and it would be wrong to treat them alike. We find out where the money actually sits, stay in gentle contact while a legitimate payer is pending, and give people the time that process genuinely takes. Only balances with no payer behind them at all are escalated.
4. What it asks of your staffVery little, which is the point. Bulk-upload an aging export from your funeral management or accounting system through our portal — we map your columns, so there is no re-keying and no new routine for directors who already have families to serve. Outreach begins within 24–48 hours of a documented placement. We also handle ongoing AR management and insurance follow-up if you would rather balances be looked after long before anyone has to call them past due.

The age curve

Why recently past-due balances recover best

A balance is most collectible while the service is still recent and the contact information is still good. Every month it ages, families move, phone numbers change, estates close and leverage erodes. The highest-leverage change most offices make isn’t chasing harder — it’s placing sooner.

The age curve

Recently past-due accounts often resolve in 30–60 days; balances that sit a year collect a fraction of that.

The standing rule

A fixed “place at 90 days” policy recovers materially more than letting balances drift toward write-off — and on contingency there’s no cost to placing.

Recovery figures vary by portfolio; SWRS gives an honest, age-based read on a representative sample before any accounts are worked.

05 — Built for deathcare receivables

We know what you are really handing us

A funeral home’s receivables are not a ledger. They are families in the middle of the hardest year of their lives, and a name that has to stay good in the neighborhood long after the balance is settled. Everything below is built around that.

Patience is the method

We move at the pace grief actually moves. Contact is gentle, spaced, and always opens with listening — because the name on that account belongs to someone who buried a person they loved, and who still lives in the community you serve.

The difference between waiting and refusing

Before a balance is ever treated as delinquent we establish whether a life-insurance assignment, probate estate or Title 19 determination is quietly working in the background — and if it is, we wait with the family rather than chase them.

Reaching the right person, not just any person

Grieving families move, change numbers and scatter across states. We take the care to reach the person who actually signed the statement of goods and services — so no one else in that family is called about a loss that is still raw.

One relationship, both locations

Milwaukee and Racine accounts worked under one agreement, one portal and one point of contact — a single person who knows your firm, not a queue that treats it as a case number.
