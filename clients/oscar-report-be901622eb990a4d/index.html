Why Balances Remain Unpaid

We reviewed the signed contracts, invoices, credit and skip-trace reviews, and the complete activity trail. No single factor explains the result. These are all of the reasons we can document, grouped so you can see which are inside Oscar’s control, which are inside ours, and which are the renters’.

Outcome to date: no recoveries on 79 accounts. The pattern is consistent across all 28 locations rather than concentrated in one dealer.
1. The renters are not reachable
  • Calls. 786 call attempts (644 automated, 142 manual). The automated Skit calls ended in voicemail (231) or no answer (227); only 2 reached a verified person and 1 a transfer. Of 172 automated dialer calls, 54 hit a machine, 31 were undeliverable, 18 hung up, 38 went unanswered, 16 were invalid numbers and 5 were refused or blocked. Only 9 connected to an agent.
  • Bad contact data. About 97 notes record disconnected, invalid or unset-up voicemail numbers, and 42 email notices bounced. 18 accounts have no delivered email at all.
  • Address changes. On 10 debts the address in ADS is in a different state from the address on the signed contract. Skip-trace updates may be correct or may point to the wrong person, and mail to a wrong address is never read.
  • Screening. Renters hang up when told the balance, let calls go to voicemail, and do not respond to letters or email. This is the main pattern in the collector notes.
2. The charges themselves invite disputes
  • Fees, not rent. On the 42 invoices we could read, 32 (76%) had already been partly paid ($18,293.02 of $58,811.97). The $40,518.95 left is mostly fees. On the 33 contracts we could match, fee lines beyond rental and coverage are $18,157.36 (38% of billed).
  • Fee types. Repossession ($250 to $1,300), late return or late delivery, smoking, cleaning, fuel, unregistered drivers, out-of-state use, damage and administrative charges. Renters contest fees they did not expect.
  • Insurance expectation. Renters told us their insurer should pay. Late return, cleaning and fuel are not insurance items, so the renter believes the bill is wrong.
  • Quoted the renter. A renter told us she was quoted about half the balance and refused to pay. Another does not recall the rental at all.
  • Chargebacks. At least one renter charged back the original card payment, which leaves the whole rental disputed.
  • Repossessions. 10 invoices include a repossession charge, which means the vehicle was not returned on time. Those renters are the least cooperative.
3. Who the renters are, and their ability to pay
  • Credit reviews. We reviewed credit on 13 accounts. The 7 with a derogatory count showed between 4 and 19 derogatory items (median 10), and 6 showed no usable credit file. None showed a healthy profile. We could not review 5 more because no Social Security number was available.
  • Age. From the 33 contracts we could read, ages run 28 to 64 (median 41): under 30, 3; 30s, 13; 40s, 13; 50 and over, 4.
  • Geography. 51 of 79 accounts are in Wisconsin. The other 28 are spread across 11 states, which makes follow-up harder.
  • Balance size. The median balance is $639. 33 accounts are under $500 and 51 are under $1,000, which is small money for a renter to prioritize paying.
  • Multiple debts. 14 of 65 renters have more than one debt, usually the same rental split between the dealer and Oscar, so a single dispute stalls several accounts.

4. Timing
  • Age at placement. Accounts reached us an average of 132 days after the newest charge. 15 were placed within 30 days, 29 within 31 to 90 days, 15 within 91 to 180 days, 14 within 181 to 365 days and 6 after a year. 35 accounts were over 90 days old when placed. Expected collectability at placement was about 70% and falls every month.
  • Time in collection. 20 accounts have been in collection about ten months with no payment. The balance of the book was placed in the last four months.
5. The file itself
  • Concentration. Three J6 Consulting accounts ($46,000.00, 42% of everything placed) are not rental invoices. Our collectors noted the service and invoice dates may not be right and asked for details. No contract or invoice for them is on file, so they cannot be pursued until the basis is documented.
  • Documentation. Some contracts are scanned images, and two placements do not match their invoices (the renter, the renter). Clean, consistent paperwork is what supports a disputed balance.
  • Duplicated balances. The same booking appears under more than one debt, so renters see charges they believe are counted twice.

  • Fees, not rent. Most of the open balance is add-on charges the renter did not expect
  • Disputes. Insurance, quoted the renter, “I don’t recall,” chargebacks
