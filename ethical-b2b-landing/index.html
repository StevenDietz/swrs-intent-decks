The short answer

Why do collection agencies have a bad reputation? Because some of them rely on pressure — relentless calls, threats, embarrassment — instead of professional conversation, and the stories about those agencies travel further than the stories about good ones. How do you choose a good one? Ask how they treat the people they contact, how they are paid, who controls legal action, and how they handle your money. Walk away from any agency that guarantees results, asks for money upfront, or will not show you its conduct standards in writing.

  • The agency speaks for you. How it behaves reflects on your company.
  • Contingency is the norm. You pay a percentage of what is recovered, not before.
  • Ask to see it, not just hear it. Letters, scripts, licenses, reports.

The reputation

Where the industry’s bad reputation comes from

Almost everyone has a story about a collection call that went wrong. The reputation is earned by a specific set of behaviors, and knowing them tells you what to look for and what to refuse.

Pressure is easy to sell and easy to measure

Some agencies are built around volume: dial as many accounts as often as possible and let the sheer number of contacts produce payments. When the only thing measured is dollars collected per call, pressure becomes the incentive, and tone, accuracy and the customer’s circumstances become obstacles.

The abuse was serious enough to need a federal law

Congress passed the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act in 1977, and the statute states its purpose plainly: to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors. The Federal Trade Commission, which enforces the law, has described debt collection as a principal subject of the consumer complaints it receives, and its reports list the harms: emotional distress, invasions of privacy, and payment of amounts that are not owed. (Sources are listed at the bottom of this page.)

A few bad actors define the whole industry

Professional agencies get lumped in with the worst ones because the public sees collection through its worst moments. A single screenshotted voicemail can undo years of goodwill, and the company that placed the account is the one customers remember.

Business debt is a different situation

Most of the stories behind the reputation involve consumer debt. The FDCPA generally covers consumer debts, meaning money owed for personal, family or household purposes, and an unpaid invoice between two businesses usually falls outside it. That does not make aggressive tactics acceptable in B2B collections. It means the conduct standards you can count on are the ones the agency sets for itself, plus whatever state law applies, so you need to ask.

Why this matters to you: your customer does not remember which agency made the call. They remember that you sent someone after them. Most past-due business customers are disputing a line item, short on cash, or have lost an invoice — people you may want to keep selling to.

Good vs. bad

What an ethical agency does differently

“Ethical” is easy to claim and hard to check, so here is what the difference looks like in practice. These are the behaviors to look for, whichever agency you evaluate.

Before you hire

Ten questions to ask any collection agency

You do not need to be an expert. You need an agency that answers these directly, in writing, without getting defensive.

  1. How do you contact my customers, and what will you never do?Listen for specifics: who is contacted, through which channels, and a clear refusal to threaten, embarrass or contact third parties.
  2. Can I see sample letters and scripts?An agency confident in its tone will show you. Read them as if you were the customer receiving them.
  3. Which licenses or bonds apply where my customers are, and can I verify them?Requirements differ by state. Confirm with the state regulator rather than relying on a logo.
  4. How are you paid, and is everything in writing?Expect contingency: a percentage of what is recovered. Ask what, if anything, you owe if nothing is recovered.
  5. Who decides whether an account goes to litigation, and who pays?The answer should be you, with a cost-and-recovery estimate in advance.
  6. How and when do I get paid what you collect?Ask about the remittance schedule, how client funds are held, and what statements you receive.
  7. How are disputes handled?A good agency documents the dispute, pauses where appropriate and routes it back to you rather than pushing harder.
  8. What does your complaint history look like?Check the Better Business Bureau, your state attorney general, and review sites. Read the negative reviews for patterns about conduct, not just ratings.
  9. How will I see what is happening on my accounts?Ask for a demonstration of the reporting or client portal before you place anything.
  10. Can I speak to a client like me?Ask for references from businesses of similar size or industry and ask them how customers were treated.
Walk away

Eight red flags

  • Guaranteed recovery ratesNo honest agency can promise what a debtor will do.
  • Upfront or unexplained feesListing, membership or “setup” fees before any recovery.
  • Pressure to sign todayAn agency that rushes you is practicing on you.
  • No written conduct standardsIf it will not tell you what it will not do, assume the worst.
  • Won’t show licenses or bondsWhere required, they should be easy to produce and verify.
  • Vague about your moneyNo clear remittance schedule or answer on how client funds are held.
  • Threatens litigation as a tacticLawsuits should be your decision, not a pressure tool.
  • Contacts third partiesRelatives, neighbors or an employer are not part of a professional approach.
Already hired one?

What to do if an agency is mistreating your customers

If you are hearing complaints about an agency working your accounts, act quickly. The agency’s conduct is being attributed to you.

  1. Document it. Collect dates, names, call logs, letters and any recordings or screenshots your customers can share.
  2. Read your agreement. Check the notice and termination terms and who controls account recalls before you act.
  3. Put your concerns in writing. Send the agency a dated written notice describing the conduct and what you require it to stop.
  4. Recall the accounts if the conduct continues. Your customers matter more than the placement.
  5. Know where to escalate. Complaints can be filed with your state attorney general and the Better Business Bureau, and consumer-debt complaints can go to the FTC and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau.

If you are unsure what your agreement allows, ask your attorney before you send anything.
